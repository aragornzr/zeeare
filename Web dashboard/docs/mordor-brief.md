# User-supplied Mordor scene brief

Both supplied attachments contain the same description. Preserved below as the design source.

Mordor, the Black Land, is a vast, fortified region in the southeastern part of Middle-earth, surrounded on three sides by immense mountain ranges. It is a land of volcanic devastation, ash-covered plains, broken stone, barren ridges, poisonous fumes, and military fortifications. Yet it is not uniformly lifeless: its southern regions contain more fertile land, worked to supply Sauron's armies.

The following description follows the geography established by J. R. R. Tolkien in The Lord of the Rings, especially The Two Towers, The Return of the King, and the maps accompanying the novels. Where Tolkien does not specify exact soil composition, geological formation, or measurements, I distinguish reasonable physical interpretation from established lore.
1. The mountain walls surrounding Mordor




















10










Mordor is protected by two great mountain systems forming an enormous, roughly rectangular enclosure, open toward the east and southeast rather than completely encircled.

The Ephel Dúath — Mountains of Shadow

These mountains form Mordor's western and southern boundaries. They are exceptionally steep, dark, and difficult to cross.

Their western slopes face Ithilien and Gondor, while their eastern slopes descend into Mordor's interior.

The mountain terrain consists of:
- High, jagged summits and long, serrated ridgelines.
- Immense rocky escarpments, cliffs, and steep slopes.
- Deep ravines, narrow gorges, and shadow-filled valleys.
- Broken rock, scree, and loose rubble on exposed slopes.
- Narrow mountain passes, some fortified with towers.
- Dark rock faces and bare mountain shoulders with little vegetation at higher elevations.
Tolkien describes the mountains as formidable natural defenses. Their exact rock types are not systematically identified. Blackened rock, fractured stone, and steep slopes are faithful visual interpretations, but describing the entire range as basalt would go beyond the text.

The Ered Lithui — Mountains of Ash

The Ered Lithui form Mordor's northern boundary, extending eastward from the region of the Black Gate.

They are characterized by rugged mountains, barren slopes, and their association with the ash and desolation of Mordor.

Their terrain can be pictured as long, uneven ridges, steep rocky faces, broken spurs, and valleys that funnel toward the interior.

The two ranges converge around Mordor's northwestern corner, creating the strategic bottleneck of Udûn.
2. The Morannon — The Black Gate of Mordor













The Morannon is the principal northern entrance to Mordor, positioned between the ends of the Ephel Dúath and Ered Lithui.

It is not merely a gate standing in an open plain. It is a fortified barrier controlling a natural opening between two immense mountain systems.

Terrain surrounding the gate

North of the Morannon lies the desolate approach from the Dagorlad, the Battle Plain. The ground is bleak, with stretches of barren earth, stone, and disturbed land. Farther northwest lie the Dead Marshes.

Immediately around the gate, the terrain is dominated by the steep mountain flanks on either side, creating a narrow defensive corridor.

The gate itself is a mighty iron barrier set into Sauron's fortifications, with great ramparts and towers.

Two major watchtowers guard the approach:
- Carchost, one of the Towers of the Teeth.
- Narchost, the other Tower of the Teeth.
These towers were originally built by Gondor to watch Mordor after Sauron's defeat at the end of the Second Age, and were later occupied by Sauron's forces.

Behind the gate lies the enclosed northern valley of Udûn.
3. Udûn — The northern valley of Mordor




















9










Udûn is a great enclosed valley or basin in northwestern Mordor, situated directly south of the Morannon.

Its position makes it one of the most strategically important parts of Sauron's realm.

Physical character

Udûn is hemmed in by the mountain systems to its west, north, and east. The surrounding heights rise steeply above the valley, making it feel like a giant natural fortress.

The landscape is dominated by:
- Rocky slopes descending from the surrounding mountains.
- Barren, uneven ground and broad valley floors.
- Broken stone, gravel, and exposed rock.
- Narrower passages between mountain spurs.
- Military roads, defensive positions, and places where armies can assemble.
The valley's ground should not be imagined as a perfectly smooth plain. The natural terrain would be uneven, with rocky projections and slopes leading down from the enclosing ranges.

However, Tolkien does not provide a detailed geological survey of Udûn, so specific descriptions of lava flows, crater walls, or exact soil layers are interpretations rather than established facts.

The Isenmouthe — Carach Angren

At Udûn's southern end lies the Isenmouthe, also called Carach Angren, the Iron Jaws.

Here, projecting mountain spurs narrow the passage leading from Udûn into the greater interior of Mordor.

It is heavily fortified, with defensive works, towers, and barriers controlling movement between the northern valley and the Plateau of Gorgoroth.

This creates a layered defense: an invading army must pass the Morannon, cross Udûn, and then penetrate the Isenmouthe before reaching the main heartland of Mordor.
4. Gorgoroth — The great plateau of Mordor




















8










Gorgoroth is the immense, desolate plateau forming the central and northwestern heart of Mordor.

It lies south of Udûn and the Isenmouthe, east of the Ephel Dúath, and beneath the northern Mountains of Ash.

This is the landscape most closely associated with Mordor's volcanic desolation.
The surface of the plateau
The plateau is broad but not entirely level. Tolkien depicts a ruined land of stony ground, ash, dust, fissures, and broken terrain.

Its principal characteristics include:

Ash and dust: Large areas are covered with fine, dark material associated with Mount Doom. Wind can lift the dust, and volcanic fumes contribute to the oppressive atmosphere.

Rocky surfaces: Exposed stone, loose fragments, sharp rubble, and barren ridges make walking difficult.

Fissures and broken ground: The land is cracked and irregular in places, with dangerous clefts and fractured surfaces.

Mounds and low ridges: Not every portion is a featureless flat expanse. The plateau includes rises, stony hummocks, slopes, and elevated ground.

Vegetation: Much of Gorgoroth is almost completely barren. Its harsh environment supports little visible plant life.
Soil and ground conditions
A plausible physical reconstruction of Gorgoroth would include several kinds of surface material.
Surface	Physical appearance
Volcanic ash	Fine, gray to black powder that shifts underfoot
Loose stone	Angular gravel and fractured rock
Compacted barren earth	Hard, dry ground, sometimes dusty
Rocky ridges	Exposed, uneven stone surfaces
Volcanic debris	Scattered larger fragments and rubble
Cracked surfaces	Broken crusts and fissured ground

This table is a geological interpretation consistent with Tolkien's volcanic imagery, not a literal soil survey provided in the books.

Gorgoroth is also an occupied military landscape. Sauron's roads and army movements connect the plateau with the Morannon, the western passes, Mount Doom, and Barad-dûr.
5. Orodruin — Mount Doom




















8










Orodruin, also called Amon Amarth or Mount Doom, rises from the Plateau of Gorgoroth, northwest of Barad-dûr.

It is Mordor's defining geological feature and the source of the volcanic power associated with Sauron's realm.
The mountain's shape
Mount Doom is a solitary volcanic mountain, rising above the surrounding plateau rather than forming part of the main boundary ranges.

It has steep upper slopes, a summit region containing the fiery chambers of the mountain, and lower flanks descending toward the plain.

The visual image of a dark volcanic cone is appropriate, although Tolkien does not prescribe a scientifically exact cone profile.
Slopes, rocks, and ash
The mountain's lower slopes are covered with ash, loose stone, and volcanic debris. Higher up, the ascent becomes increasingly difficult.

The mountain's terrain includes:
- Loose ash that makes footing uncertain.
- Sharp, broken volcanic-looking rocks.
- Steep and unstable slopes.
- Sulfurous fumes, heat, and choking air.
- Fiery openings and molten rock within the mountain.
- Dangerous ground near the upper volcanic chambers.
The region around Mount Doom is especially hostile because of its heat, smoke, and volcanic activity.
The Road to the Sammath Naur
One of the most important constructed features of Mount Doom is Sauron's road ascending the mountain.

It leads toward the Sammath Naur, the Chambers of Fire, where the One Ring was forged.

The road winds up the mountain and provides access to the dark doorway of the chamber.

Within the Sammath Naur lies the Crack of Doom, the fiery chasm into which the Ring must be cast.

The road is important geographically because it shows that Mount Doom is not merely an inaccessible volcano. It is connected to the rest of Mordor by a deliberately constructed route.
6. Barad-dûr — The Dark Tower













Barad-dûr, the Dark Tower, stands in northeastern Gorgoroth, east of Mount Doom.

It is Sauron's principal stronghold, not a natural mountain, although its colossal size and dark architecture make it seem like an extension of the surrounding landscape.
The terrain around Barad-dûr
The fortress stands in the region of the Ered Lithui's southern reaches, above the barren plains of Gorgoroth.

The landscape around it is dark, stony, and desolate, with roads and fortifications connecting the tower to the rest of Sauron's military realm.

The tower is associated with the dark stronghold and defenses of the region, rather than standing as a solitary building without supporting infrastructure.
Architecture and defenses
Tolkien's Barad-dûr is a colossal fortress of many walls, towers, battlements, and vast foundations.

It is described through impressions of enormous height, shadow, iron, stone, and overwhelming strength.

Its foundations were made with the power of the One Ring and could not be destroyed while the Ring endured.

The famous cinematic version depicts a towering black spire with the fiery Eye of Sauron at its summit. That is a film interpretation; the novels do not establish a literal giant flaming eyeball permanently mounted above the tower.

Likewise, exact heights, floor counts, wall thicknesses, and architectural plans are not specified by Tolkien.
7. Cirith Ungol — The high pass in the Mountains of Shadow




















8










Cirith Ungol lies in the western mountain wall of Mordor, above the Morgul Vale.

It is one of the most dangerous approaches into the Black Land.

The region is not a broad road crossing an ordinary mountain saddle. It is a narrow, treacherous route through precipitous heights, steep stairs, and rocky passages.
The approach from the west
Travelers approaching from Ithilien enter the Morgul Vale, overshadowed by Minas Morgul.

From there, the route toward Cirith Ungol climbs into the Ephel Dúath.

The terrain becomes increasingly steep, confined, and dangerous.

The route includes the Stairs of Cirith Ungol, with their difficult ascents, before reaching the region of Shelob's Lair.
Shelob's Lair
Shelob inhabits a network of dark passages within the mountains.

The passages are suffocating, lightless, and filled with webs. They provide a route through the mountain barrier, but one controlled by an ancient and deadly creature.
The Tower of Cirith Ungol
The Tower of Cirith Ungol stands near the pass, guarding this approach into Mordor.

Like the Towers of the Teeth, it was originally built by Gondor after Sauron's overthrow to watch the borders of Mordor.

It later became an Orc-held fortress.

Its position on the high rocky terrain gives it command over the pass and the surrounding approaches.
Rock and ridge structure
For a physically plausible reconstruction, imagine sheer cliffs, exposed rocky ribs, loose stones on narrow ledges, deep ravines, and ridges rising above the mountain track.

The main danger is the topography itself: restricted footing, great drops, confined passages, and the impossibility of easily escaping the route.
8. The interior road system and military geography
Mordor is not an empty wilderness. It is a militarized territory with roads, strongholds, camps, supply routes, and controlled passes.

Its geography creates a defensive hierarchy.
